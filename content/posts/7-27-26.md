> Disclaimer: this post contains spoilers for Christopher Nolan's 2026 depiction
> of The Odyssey. If you are planning to watch the movie, I would recommend
> doing so prior to reading, even if you are already familiar with the story.

I've spent most of my career leading teams, including serving as CTO at
[Golioth](https://golioth.io/) prior to our acquisition by
[Canonical](https://canonical.com/). Over the years I've tried to commit a
significant portion of my time to listening to feedback from individuals on
those teams, reflecting on my own decisions and motivations, and seeking advice
and guidance from those who have been successful leaders.

I am also a diehard Christopher Nolan fan, with four of my top five favorite
movies being Nolan films (and yes, one of them is
[Tenet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenet)). I've been looking forward to [The
Odyssey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Odyssey_(2026_film)) since the
announcement of its release date, and I finally got the opportunity to go and
see it in all of its [IMAX
glory](https://www.odysseymovie.com/explore-formats/).

> Well, most of its IMAX glory; we don't have a 70mm IMAX theater anywhere in
> North Carolina so a 1.90:1 aspect ratio had to suffice.

I went into the movie with high expectations, but I wasn't planning on taking
away new lessons from the experience. After all, the story has been retold for
more than 2,500 years. Nevertheless, the choices in Nolan's screenplay, combined
with encountering it as my 30-year-old self rather than my 16 year-old-self,
left me pondering the story's concepts in a new light over the following days.
While [Odysseus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odysseus) is many things, perhaps
his most defining trait is his leadership. Throughout the Odyssey, he is
constantly making decisions that have a profound direct impact on the soldiers
he is leading, but also indirectly impact his family, country, and all of
society.

In many ways, Odysseus plays a sort of CTO role. He both devises technical plans
and implements them alongside his men, but ultimately answers to
[Agamemnon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agamemnon), his hot-headed CEO. In
other words, he leads from the front. However, like many hand-on leaders when
faced with constraints, he can fall into the trap of thinking that he can
overcome obstacles through sheer will or his own cleverness. This manifests as
both acts of hubris and an inability to accept hard truths at various points
throughout the story.

Odysseus's successes and failures highlight a few leadership lessons that I have
learned throughout my own journey, many times through painful failures.

### Respect is Earned, Continually

> **Odysseus:** _"Is this a mutiny?"_

> **Eurylochus:** _"It's up to you."_

After the victory in the Trojan War, Odysseus has undoubtedly cemented his
legacy with the success of the Trojan Horse scheme. You would think that there
is nothing that he could do to lose respect, especially from the men who
witnessed his exploits in person. However, in a moment of pride, Odysseus fires
an arrow at Poseidon's son, Polyphemus, after he and his men had already escaped
entrapment in the giant's cave.

> Note that Nolan chooses this act of hubris rather than having Odysseus
> disclose his name, as is [the case in the original
> poem](https://www.reddit.com/r/ChristopherNolan/comments/1v04bpb/the_odyssey_cutting_this_scene_from_the_book/)).

A chase back to the boats ensues, and the men come to believe that their
subsequent misfortune is a result of Odysseus offending Poseidon. Whether this
is true or not, it eventually outweighs Odysseus's prior legendary feats, and
results in the exchange about mutiny and the men requesting that he remain at
shore when they arrive at [Aeaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeaea). It is a
visceral illustration of the fact that a leader can never rest on their laurels.
Their team will and should demand that they demonstrate poise and composure at
all times. Failure to do so comes with consequences.

### Let Your Team Make Mistakes

> **Circe:** _"Leave your weapons outside."_

Following the subdued mutiny on the beach of Aeaea, Odysseus does not impede his
men's trek up the hill as he goes off to hunt for food. Along the way, the men
encounter some very out of place animals, such as a lion and a jaguar, that
foreshadow the events that readers of The Odyssey already know are about to
transpire. Upon reaching a clearly inhabited hut, the men encounter
[Circe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circe), who acts frightened by their
arrival. Nevertheless, she agrees to feed them as long as they remove their
armor and weapons and place them behind the hut.

In their hunger, the men look past the pile of rusting armor evidently belonging
to previous soldiers who had been presented with the same prompt. The subsequent
scene is by far the most disturbing in the movie, as Circe takes a very...
_hands on_ approach in transforming the men into pigs. When Odysseus later makes
his way to the hut, he notices all of the signals that his men so conveniently
overlooked. He shrewdly identifies Circe's bird as someone she cares about in
animal form, and uses it as leverage to have her transform the pigs back into
his men.

> We also get one of the quotes of the movie when Circe yells _"Back into your
> disguises!"_ as the pigs retake the shape of the men.

It is not a stretch to think that Odysseus could have saved his men from this
dreadful experience had he insisted on accompanying them initially. Had he known
the pain they were destined to experience, he may have been more persistent.
However, there is value in not standing in the way of individuals who wish to
assume more responsibility. They may demonstrate the ability to lead, to both
others and themselves, or they may learn a valuable lesson that shapes how they
approach future leadership opportunities. Too much protection at best slows
their growth, and at worst stunts it.

### Be Tactful with Information

> **Eurylochus:** _"Why didn't you tell us?!?"_

After the encounter with Circe at Aeaea, Odysseus and his men sail to Hades,
where Odysseus has a private conversation with
[Tiresias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiresias). Tiresias informs Odysseus
that they will have to choose between sailing into a whirlpool
([Charybdis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charybdis)) or by a six-headed
monster ([Scylla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scylla)). The former would
result in the entire crew being lost, whereas the latter would mean that six men
would die.

As they leave Hades, Odysseus wrestles with the decision whether to tell his men
of the prophecy or not. He eventually decides not to, and even goes one step
further to command them to sail into the whirlpool, an order he knows they will
defy, resulting in passing by Scylla and losing six crew members. Odysseus not
only withholds the information for his men, but manipulates them into making the
decision that he believes to be correct. In doing so, he implicitly makes the
decision for them, while avoiding taking ownership of doing so.

Though withholding for the purpose of manipulation is a fairly obvious negative
leadership trait, choosing when and how to disclose information is a decision
that leaders are confronted with on a daily basis. Early on in my career I would
characterize myself as an "information transparency maximalist", opting to
frequently share virtually all information I received with all members of the
team. Some of this was likely borne out of personal insecurity, or perhaps even
a lack of accountability (i.e. telling everyone everything can act as a
diffusion of responsibility). I would still consider myself as a leader that
biases towards transparency, but I have learned more about the value of
compressing or synthesizing information. After all, if you act as a strict
passthrough, you are introducing communication overhead without adding value.

Setting aside the manipulation of his crew, perhaps Odysseus can reconcile the
decision to sail by Scylla by saying that he was optimizing for the best outcome
for the greatest number of people. In all organizational structures, there is an
exchanging of personal agency for efficiency. It is important that there is
explicit alignment on the amount of agency that is being surrendered to the
leader. The leader is entrusted to not overstep that agreement.

I won't pretend to understand the contract between
[Achaean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaeans_(Homer)) soldiers and their
generals. I think it is safe to assume that they are surrendering more agency
than most of us would ever be comfortable with. Nevertheless, I wouldn't be
surprised if Odysseus felt some regret in how transparent he was about the
prophecy. There is some evidence that this event caused him to recalibrate, as
he later informs the crew of the part of the prophecy that states that if they
eat the cattle of the sun god they will all be killed by Zeus.

Most of the vast majority of decisions that leaders make are not as
consequential as Odysseus's predicament. However, his scenario provides a useful
test for whether it is better to share information with a team or not: how would
they react if they were to learn of the information later? This typically has a
high correlation to the personal impact that the information has on each team
member. In this case, the impact was death for some of the crew, and extreme
guilt for the remaining members. I'd say that is pretty significant!

### Continue to Refine Yourself

> **Odysseus:** _"Put the wax in the men's ears. None for me. Tie me to the
> mast."_

In one of the most famous stories from The Odyssey, Odysseus, on the advice of
Circe, instructs his men to fill their ears with wax as they sail by the Sirens,
so as not to be drawn in by their intoxicating song. However, he refuses the wax
for himself, instead requesting that the men bind him to the mast so that he can
hear the song of the Sirens, but not fall into the trap of pursuing them. This
category of directive has become known in modern times as a [Ulysses
pact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulysses_pact).

The men don't directly gain anything from Odysseus hearing the song of the
Sirens, and at first it may seem like an ego-driven decision. If he truly
prioritized the crew, would he incur the risk of either being driven mad by the
song, or somehow breaking free from the mast and sailing into their trap?
Perhaps, but the fact that Odysseus survives suggests that he has a strong grasp
of his own limitations and believes that the wisdom he will gain from the
experience far outweighs the risk. Or maybe he just couldn't pass up the
opportunity to be the first person who heard the Sirens and lived. We can never
know for sure.

What we do know, and what is depicted so vividly in Nolan's rendition, is the
extreme torment that Odysseus underwent in hearing the song while being
restrained. We also know what the song was about, courtesy of
[Eurylochus's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurylochus) inquiry.

> **Odysseus:** _"All the things you want it to be. Then, all the things you
> wish you'd never wish for. It was the delicious itch you go to scratch but
> find it’s under the skin, you can't reach it. So the delicious itch becomes
> unbearable. Told you what you most want is what you most can't have. And what
> you most can’t have is what you already had and lost. It was the song of all
> the promises I failed to keep. And it told me I don't really want to go
> home."_

As a leader, it can be easy to always see the best option as what prioritizes
your team. However, if you do not take the opportunity to also refine yourself,
you will be a less effective leader for them in the long run. Odysseus is
attentive to the opportunity that he has to refine his understanding of the
world they are living in and the role they played in creating it. This moment
may be the point at which Odysseus first begins to recognize the irreversible
impact of the actions he and his men carried out in Troy. He understands that,
though they all long to be home, the home they are returning to will never be
the one that they left. This knowledge guides him throughout the remainder of
his journey home.

### Do Not Win at All Costs

> **Odysseus:** _"What if when he left the belly of the horse and opened the
> gates of Troy he saw ten years of rage pour into that city in one night. We
> left them a gift in offering of peace that they took into their home. We
> violated all that's ever sacred between people and turned the fight into a
> hunt. To burn the walls of Troy was to burn the world entirely, including his
> home. What if he knew, that very night as he walked through fires of anarchy
> and pain. And in the days of sweaty celebrations that followed, what if he
> knew exactly what he'd done?"_

> **Odysseus:** _"One man's idea, one man's trick to break Zeus's Law forever.
> We live in a world of palaces, trades, language, blinded with beauty until we
> broke it."_

Odysseus's monologue upon being reunited with Penelope codifies the insight that
began to take shape when he heard the song of the Sirens. What the world sees as
his greatest triumph, he has come to realize is actually his greatest failure.
He has destroyed the [precedent of treating strangers with
respect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenia_(Greek)), which has created a
domino effect, causing society as a whole to crumble.

It is possible that Odysseus and his men did not understand the value of the
civilization in which they lived, and because of this they were flippant in
their protection of it. I think it is more likely that they were blinded by
their ten years of suffering and their desire to return home to their families.
This led them to adopting an any-means-necessary mindset, where no cost was too
great on the way to securing their victory.

The more resources you invest in any effort, whether it be time, energy, or
money, the easier it can be to fall prey to tunnel vision. When you lose the
ability to step back and look at the big picture, you can end up compromising
principles that your past self would have considered non-negotiable. In the end,
the cost of the compromise is so great that you look back and wonder how you
ever thought that the trade-offs were worth it.

Many of Odysseus's men end up losing their lives despite their victory in Troy.
Agamemnon [faces
reprocussions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agamemnon#Return_to_Greece_and_death)
of his own brash decision-making. Odysseus and the others who survive are forced
to live the rest of their days longing for the thing they most can't have, the
thing they already had and lost.

Though Odysseus does make it back to his wife and son, I don't think it is a
stretch to say that he may have chosen to die in Troy rather than enacting his
scheme if he were given the opportunity to do it over again.

## Is Odysseus a Good Leader?

We've established that Odysseus leads, but does he lead well? To address the
elephant in the room... his stats are not great. All of the men he is
responsible for end up dying, and an uncomfortably straight line can be drawn
from his questionable decision-making to their deaths. Many of the leadership
lessons described in this post are illustrated using Odysseus as a cautionary
tale, rather than an exemplar of best practices. It is frustrating, though
perhaps not unfamiliar, for a person to be held up as a hero due to a single
act, when in reality that act is surrounded by a litany of failures.

While Odysseus's recognition of the harm his great triumph set in motion does
not negate his short-comings, I do believe that the best leaders are able to
identify when the thing that they are praised for is actually something that
requires reform. In the startup world, a classic example of this scenario is
when a company transitions from a small group acting as heroes to a larger
organization where it is optimal for knowledge and workload to be distributed
across many individuals. I myself have been guilty of holding too tightly to
roles and responsibilities that I needed to surrender.

Ultimately the answer to whether someone is a good leader depends on the
attributes that you value most highly. If flaw minimization is your goal,
Odysseus does not stack up well. However, if you are looking for someone who
will continually examine their own motivations and is willing and able to accept
their mistakes, he certainly demonstrates those qualities. Maybe I gravitate
towards Odysseus because I know that I am deeply flawed, yet I still believe
that I have something to offer as a leader. But perhaps I really just want to be
the person that heard the song of the Sirens and lived.
